Ball Party — three sports-media directions

Round two, pulled from sports media rather than physical objects: an on-air broadcast, a streaming network, and a live-scores app. Same three screens each — home or lobby, the Wavelength guessing round, the scoring moment — with the same state throughout: room BLTZ, round 3 of 8, Kayla is the psychic, the clue is “Gronk”, the needle lands in a 3.

Direction 04

The Broadcast

You’re not playing a game, you’re on air. Score bug, lower thirds, cut-ins, the graphics package that wraps every televised match. The signature is that the app narrates itself: every event — a psychic assigned, a clue submitted, a needle locked, a score — slides in as a chyron, and the score bug never leaves the bottom of the screen. It turns four minigames into one continuous broadcast, and it solves your idle-player problem directly: the two players with nothing to do are watching coverage.

Direction 05

The Network

Ball Party is a channel and each minigame is a show with its own brand. The signature is the rundown — the home screen is a programming schedule, not a menu grid, and Party Mode is “tonight’s card” with an on-now, up-next and a running order. This is the direction that answers a real product question: how do you present four unrelated minigames and three modes without a shrug of a menu? It also converts the dead air between minigames into “up next, starting in eight seconds”, which is the seam Mario-Party-style modes usually fumble.

Direction 06

The Sports App

The live-scores app your friends already have on their home screen — a matchup at the top, a play-by-play underneath, tabs at the bottom. The signature is the matchup header: team, score, period, score, team. It’s the most recognisable object in all of sports interface, it reads instantly as a live game, and it fits 2v2 Wavelength exactly. The play-by-play feed underneath is the other half — every clue, guess and point becomes a timestamped entry, which is both what idle players read and what you screenshot into the group chat afterwards.

Reading the three

The Network is the strongest idea, and it’s the one that isn’t really about looks. The rundown answers the question your docs leave open — how Party Mode, Arcade and Solo coexist without a menu — and giving each minigame its own show colour and title card lets four unrelated games feel like one channel instead of one app with a theming problem.

The Broadcast is the most fun and the most expensive. Every event needs a chyron, which means every game needs an authored line of commentary copy, and that only works if you commit to writing them well. If you do, it’s the most distinctive product here.

The Sports App is the safest and the most legible, and it would be the right call if the audience is closer to the group chat that argues about box scores than to a party. Its risk is that it reads as a companion app: correct, familiar, and not much of an occasion.

If you want one to build: the Network shell with the Broadcast’s chyron as the in-game event layer. The platform framing carries the navigation problem, and the chyron supplies the noise a party needs at the moments that matter.
