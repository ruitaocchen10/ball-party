Ball Party — three neutral dark directions

Restrained: a neutral dark base, a grey text ramp, and exactly one accent hue each. What changes between them is form — flat versus layered versus ruled — and where the one accent is allowed to appear. Same three screens and same state throughout: room BLTZ, round 3 of 8, Kayla is the psychic, clue “Gronk”, the needle lands in a 3. Contrast checked per theme: body text ≥ 4.5:1, secondary ≥ 3:1, targets ≥ 48px, tabular numerals on every score and clock.

Direction 07

Graphite

Flat and typographic. No cards, no fills, no shadows — hierarchy comes only from type scale, weight, and a single hairline rule. It’s the least decorated of the three and the fastest to build, because there is almost nothing to build.

Accent rule: amber appears on the clock and the points, and nowhere else. Those are the two things that create tension in every one of your four games, so the one colour in the product is reserved for time and score.

Direction 08

Carbon

Layered surfaces on a cool near-black — cards, hairline borders, 14px radii, a consistent two-step elevation scale. This is the one that looks like a shipped product app rather than a design exercise, and it is the most forgiving as screens get busier, because new content gets a surface instead of more type contrast.

Accent rule: blue means “you can touch this”. The needle you drag and the primary button are blue; nothing decorative ever is. The bullseye is plain white, so the reveal reads as information rather than as another button.

Direction 09

Slate

Ruled and tabular. Nothing is filled; hairline rules do all the separating, corners are square, and numbers live in a monospace column that lines up. It reads as a results service — the closest of the three to something a person who actually follows sport would trust.

Accent rule: red only ever means live or urgent — the on-air dot, and the clock once it drops under five seconds. It never marks a score, a button, or a heading, so when red appears something is genuinely happening.

Reading the three

Carbon is what I’d build. Surfaces absorb complexity that flat layouts can’t — when the auction needs a bid list and a timer and six lots on one screen, Graphite starts fighting itself and Carbon just adds a card. The accent rule (blue = touchable) is also the one of the three that keeps working as the product grows, because it’s a functional rule rather than an aesthetic one.

Graphite is the most confident-looking and the cheapest to ship, and it would be the right pick if you want to get all four games playable before you think about visual identity. Its ceiling is lower: everything is type, so a dense screen has nowhere to go.

Slate is the most distinctive of the three and the coldest. The tables are a real advantage — player records in the lobby, by-round scoring, arcade leaderboards all come free — but nothing about it says “party”. Worth choosing deliberately, not by default.

All three share the same token names (--bg, --fg, --fg2, --line, --accent), so they’re swappable — you can build against one and re-skin later by changing five values.
